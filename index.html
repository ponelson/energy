01  /  First principles

What energy is, in six equations

The rest of this page counts exajoules without ever saying what one is. Energy is a single quantity with dimensions of mass times length squared over time squared, and every form it takes on this page is the same quantity wearing different clothes. Work is energy transferred by a force. Power is the rate. Mass is energy at rest. And in general relativity energy is not a scalar you can simply add up, it is the source term that curves spacetime.

Energy, work, heat, power

Four words that are not synonyms

Energy is a state function: a system has it. Dimensions M L² T⁻², unit the joule.
Work is energy transferred by a force acting through a distance, W = F·d. A process, not a possession.
Heat is energy transferred by a temperature difference. Also a process. The first law is just bookkeeping over the two: ΔU = Q − W.
Power is the rate, P = dE/dt, unit the watt. This is the distinction that makes a battery's two numbers necessary: kilowatts is how fast, kilowatt-hours is how much.

Why energy is conserved

Noether's theorem, which is the real answer

Energy conservation is not an empirical accident. In 1918 Emmy Noether proved that every continuous symmetry of a physical system implies a conserved quantity, and the symmetry that gives energy is invariance under translation in time: the laws are the same today as tomorrow.

The uncomfortable corollary is that on cosmological scales, where the universe is expanding and time-translation symmetry does not hold globally, total energy is not well defined. Conservation of energy is a local theorem, not a universal accounting identity.

Mass

E = mc², and the number that follows from it

Mass is energy in the rest frame. One kilogram is 8.99×1016 joules, about 21.5 megatonnes of TNT, or roughly a tenth of what humanity uses in a day.

Fission converts about 0.09% of the mass of its fuel; chemical combustion converts on the order of 10−10. That ratio, about a million to one, is the entire argument for nuclear energy density, and it is why a reactor's fuel cost is trivial and its capital cost is everything.

Relativity

Energy is not invariant, and this matters more than it sounds

Energy and momentum are components of one four-vector, related by E² = (pc)² + (mc²)². Different observers measure different energies for the same object; what they agree on is the invariant mass. So "the energy of a system" is always implicitly "in this frame."

In general relativity the source of gravity is not mass but the full stress-energy tensor, through Gμν = 8πG/c⁴ · Tμν. Pressure and energy flux gravitate too. Spacetime curvature is what energy does when there is enough of it in one place.

Quantum

Energy comes in packets, and that sets the solar limit

E = hf = hc/λ. A green photon at 550 nm carries 3.6×10−19 J, or 2.25 eV. Silicon has a band gap of 1.1 eV, so every photon above that wastes the excess as heat and every photon below it passes through unabsorbed.

Those two losses are why a single-junction cell cannot exceed about 33% under one sun, the Shockley-Queisser limit, and why tandem cells that stack band gaps are the only route past it. The physics of the solar section is contained in this one equation.

Thermodynamics

Where the grey flows on this page come from

The second law says a heat engine between reservoirs at Th and Tc cannot beat η = 1 − Tc/Th. A supercritical steam plant at 600℃ rejecting to 27℃ has a ceiling of about 66%, and real fleets run near 39%.

So the 337 exajoules of rejected energy in the flow diagram are not incompetence, they are mostly Carnot. The better framing is exergy: the useful work available in a joule. Electricity is nearly pure exergy; 40℃ water is nearly none. Electrification wins because it stops converting high-exergy fuel into low-exergy heat on the way to a service that only needed the heat.

02  /  First principles

Amps, volts, watts: four numbers, one equation, and a hundred-year argument

The previous section defined energy. This one defines the only form of it that can be moved a thousand kilometres in a hundredth of a second, and explains the two decisions that shaped every wire on this page: why we push electricity at absurd voltages, and why we make it wobble back and forth instead of flowing in one direction. Both answers are single equations.

The electrical quantities, and where they meet the joule
Everything here is definitional. Voltage is joules per coulomb, current is coulombs per second, so their product is joules per second, which is the watt. That is the whole of it.
The four quantities

What each one actually is

Charge, Q, coulombs. The conserved stuff. One coulomb is about 6.24×1018 electrons.
Current, I, amps. Charge per second. I = dQ/dt. One amp is one coulomb per second.
Voltage, V, volts. Energy per unit charge, one joule per coulomb. Not a thing that flows, a difference in potential between two points. There is no such thing as the voltage at a place, only between places.
Resistance, R, ohms. How much voltage it takes to push a given current. V = IR.

Multiply the first two rates together and you have power: P = VI, joules per coulomb times coulombs per second, which cancels to joules per second. A 100 W bulb on 120 V draws 0.83 A.

The analogy, and where it fails

Water pressure is useful for about ninety seconds

Voltage as pressure, current as flow rate, resistance as pipe narrowness, and power as the rate work gets done. That gets you a long way and it is how most people usefully think about it.

Where it misleads: the electrons barely move. Drift velocity in a household wire is on the order of a fraction of a millimetre per second, slower than a walking ant. The energy travels at close to the speed of light, in the electromagnetic field around the conductor, not in the charges themselves. The wire is a guide for a field, not a pipe for a fluid. Which is also why a break anywhere stops everything instantly rather than draining slowly.

The cheat sheet

Six relations that cover almost everything

P = V · I
V = I · R
P = I² · R
P = V² / R
E = P · t
Q = I · t

The third one, P = I²R, is the most consequential line in the history of electrical engineering, because loss rises with the square of current. Halve the current and you quarter the waste. Everything about how the grid is built follows from that exponent.

Why AC won in 1890

One device, and it only works on alternating current

Faraday's law says a changing magnetic flux induces a voltage. A transformer is two coils on an iron core, and the ratio of turns sets the ratio of voltages, with no moving parts, no losses worth mentioning, and nothing to wear out. It is arguably the most efficient machine ever built, routinely above 99%.

But it requires change. Steady direct current produces steady flux, which induces nothing. In the 1880s there was no way to change the voltage of DC at any useful scale, which meant Edison's direct current had to be generated at roughly the voltage it was consumed at, and therefore within about a mile of the load. Westinghouse and Tesla could step up to transmit and step down to deliver. That single asymmetry decided the architecture of every grid on earth, and it is why the voltage staircase in the chain-of-custody section has the shape it does.

Why DC is coming back

Power electronics removed the reason AC won

Mercury-arc valves, then thyristors, then insulated-gate bipolar transistors made it possible to convert DC voltages by switching very fast rather than by induction. Once that existed, DC's advantages became available again, and there are four:

No charging current. An AC cable is a capacitor; it draws current to charge and discharge itself sixty times a second. Beyond roughly 80 to 100 km of subsea cable, that current consumes the entire rating and the cable can carry no useful power at all. DC has no such limit, so every long subsea link is DC by necessity rather than preference.
Lower loss over distance. Two conductors instead of three, no skin effect, and the full cross-section carries current.
No synchronism required. A DC link joins two grids that are not in step, which is exactly how Texas connects to its neighbours without becoming part of them.
Controllable flow. AC power goes wherever impedance sends it. A DC link is dispatchable to the megawatt, which is why it can be sold as a product.

Three phases

Why three, and not one or six

Three windings 120 degrees apart produce something remarkable: the instantaneous total power is constant, even though each individual phase pulses. A single-phase motor shudders twice per cycle; a three-phase motor pulls smoothly. The same three currents also produce a rotating magnetic field for free, which is what makes the induction motor possible, and induction motors consume something close to half of all electricity generated.

Three is also the minimum number that achieves this, and in a balanced system the return currents cancel so the neutral carries almost nothing. That saves roughly a quarter of the copper compared with three separate circuits. More phases would add conductor for no further benefit, which is why nobody uses six.

Root mean square

Why a 230 V outlet peaks at 325 V

An AC voltage is a sine wave swinging positive and negative, so its average is zero, which is useless for describing it. Instead we use the root mean square: square it, average that, take the root. RMS is defined precisely so that V²/R gives the correct average power, which means an RMS volt does the same heating work as a DC volt.

For a sine wave, RMS is the peak divided by √2. So nominal 230 V actually swings to ±325 V, and 120 V to ±170 V. Insulation has to survive the peak while the power calculation uses the RMS, and that gap is a real design cost of alternating current.

Reactive power

The current that transfers no energy and still costs you

If voltage and current are out of phase, part of the current sloshes back and forth without delivering net energy. Real power in watts is VI·cosφ, apparent power in volt-amps is VI, and the difference is reactive power in VAR. Motors and long lines both create it.

It matters because that useless current is real current: it still causes I²R heating, still occupies conductor, and still needs generator capacity. That is why industrial tariffs bill on kVA rather than kW, why substations have capacitor banks, and why a data centre's UPS specification is in kVA. Reactive power is also the thing that holds voltage up, and it does not travel far, so it has to be produced locally, which is one of the invisible services the grid section describes.

The accidents

Fifty, sixty, one-twenty, two-thirty

AEG in Germany picked 50 Hz because it suited their metric-sized generator; Westinghouse settled on 60 Hz partly to reduce visible lamp flicker. Neither is meaningfully better, and the split is now permanent because everything downstream is built around it.

Household voltage is a genuine engineering trade. Europe's 230 V needs half the current for the same power, so thinner wire and less loss, but it is substantially more dangerous to touch. America's 120 V is safer per contact and doubles the copper in the walls, then recovers the difference by bringing 240 V split-phase to the panel for anything large. Japan runs 100 V and, uniquely, both frequencies, split down the middle of the country by which manufacturer sold the first generators.

The irony worth ending on

The grid is now AC sitting between DC sources and DC loads

Solar panels produce DC. Batteries store DC. Fuel cells make DC. Wind turbines generate variable-frequency AC that is rectified to DC before anything useful happens to it. And on the demand side, LEDs, every processor, every phone charger, every variable-speed motor drive and every server rack in a data centre runs on DC internally.

So the modern chain is DC, converted to AC, transmitted, converted back to DC, with conversion losses at each step and a hundred-year-old architecture in the middle that exists because in 1890 nobody could change the voltage of direct current. The transformer solved a problem that power electronics has now also solved, which is why DC microgrids, DC data centre distribution, and continental HVDC are all live engineering arguments rather than curiosities. thesis

06  /  Hydrocarbons

Extraction: four different businesses that happen to sell the same molecule

A Ghawar production well and a Permian lateral both make crude oil, but they are opposite financial instruments. One is a bond with a 40-year tenor. The other is a manufacturing line with an 80% first-year decline that must be rebuilt every quarter.

Conventional onshore · ~55% of supply

Pressure-driven, long-lived, politically held

Reservoir pressure or waterflood drives flow through perforations. Decline is 5–10% a year, so a field is a multi-decade annuity. CapEx $5–20M per well, lifting cost often under $10/bbl. This is the asset class OPEC+ quota discipline actually controls, and the reason a production cut is credible: the barrels can wait.

Ghawar · Rumaila · Samotlor · Burgan

Tight oil · ~10% of global, ~65% of US

Manufacturing, not exploration

Hydraulic fracturing plus 2–4 km laterals in rock with millidarcy permeability. Per-well decline is 65–80% in year one, so output is a function of the current rig count, not the reserve base. Half-cycle breakeven $40–55/bbl. Consequence: US supply responds to price in months rather than years, which caps rallies and puts a soft floor under OPEC+ pricing power.

Permian · Bakken · Eagle Ford · Vaca Muerta · Montney

Offshore and deepwater

Long-cycle capital with political tail risk

Tension-leg platforms, spars, and semi-submersibles over subsea trees, manifolds, and umbilicals. $10–50B per development, 5–8 years from final investment decision to first oil, and a break-even that usually needs $50–70/bbl sustained. Because the decision horizon exceeds the political horizon, this is the segment that transition risk actually shrinks first.

Gulf of Mexico · Guyana Stabroek · Campos · Johan Sverdrup · Angola block 17

Enhanced recovery

The only place CO₂ already has a market price

Thermal (SAGD), chemical (polymer), and miscible CO₂ flooding lift recovery from roughly 30% of oil in place to 40–60%. CO₂-EOR matters beyond production: it is the one carbon-capture use case with an established revenue stream, and a share of injected CO₂ stays put. That makes it both a decarbonization on-ramp and a fair target for accusations of greenwashing.

Complexity as a business model

Nelson index is the whole story

Topping (NCI 2–4): distillation only, sells fuel oil, dies first in any margin squeeze.
Conversion (NCI 6–8): adds FCC, captures the gasoline spread.
Full conversion (NCI 10–14+): adds hydrocracking, coking, alkylation. Buys the cheapest heavy sour crude and sells the most valuable light products. This is the US Gulf Coast, Jamnagar, and Jurong.
Integrated refinery-petrochemical: a steam cracker on the fence turns naphtha into olefins, decoupling revenue from transport fuel demand entirely. Every new Asian and Middle Eastern build is this configuration.

Where the margin comes from

Crack spreads and the closure logic

The 3-2-1 crack, three barrels of crude against two of gasoline and one of distillate, typically runs $5–20/bbl and is the industry's clock. Cash costs of $3–6/bbl mean a plant keeps running well below its full-cost recovery, so closures happen late and then all at once.

Global capacity is roughly 103 Mb/d against about 84 Mb/d of runs. The overhang sits in aging Atlantic-basin plants that would need $0.5–2B of environmental and hydrogen capital to stay compliant. That capital decision, not demand, is what closes European and Australian refineries.

08  /  Hydrocarbons

Logistics and pricing: the barrel is a location, not a substance

Brent, WTI, Dubai, and Urals are the same commodity separated by sulfur, gravity, and a shipping route. Almost every energy crisis of the last five years has been a transport crisis wearing a commodity costume.

Benchmarks

Four prices, one market

Brent ~38°API, 0.4% S. Waterborne, prices roughly two thirds of world crude.
WTI ~40°API, 0.24% S. Landlocked at Cushing until the export ban lifted; now Midland-based cargoes tie it to Brent.
Dubai/Oman the medium sour marker for Asian term contracts.
Urals ~32°API, 1.5% S. Its discount to Brent is now a sanctions instrument rather than a quality signal.

Gas is not one market

Three prices, no arbitrage below LNG cost

Henry Hub typically $2–5/MMBtu; European TTF and Asian JKM trade at a multiple of it because the link between them is a liquefaction train, not a pipeline. Liquefaction plus shipping plus regasification costs roughly $3–5/MMBtu, and that spread is the ceiling on convergence. 2022 proved the other direction: when the pipeline disappears, the spread goes to whatever scarcity demands, TTF touched the equivalent of $90/MMBtu.

Cost curve

Who sets the marginal barrel

Middle East conventional under $20/bbl · Russian onshore $25–40 · US tight oil $40–55 · deepwater $45–70 · oil sands mining $55–75 · new Arctic $75+. Price is set at the right-hand side of the curve, and the left-hand side collects the rent. Every OPEC+ decision is an attempt to choose which part of the curve clears.

Modes

Pipeline, hull, rail, truck

Trunk pipelines move 1–4 Mb/d at $1–3/bbl per thousand kilometres and are the cheapest and least flexible option. Tankers are the opposite: VLCCs at 2 Mbbl per hull turn the ocean into a re-routable, storable pipeline at $1–5/bbl depending on freight cycles. Product tankers carry segregated parcels so that jet fuel and gasoline never touch. Rail and truck exist for the last 200 km and for arbitrage that pipelines cannot reach.

Inventory as policy

Reserves, contango, and floating storage

The US SPR holds up to 714 Mbbl in salt caverns and released 180 Mbbl in 2022, the largest intervention in its history. China's state and commercial stocks are larger and far less transparent. Commercially, storage is a term structure trade: contango pays you to hold barrels, backwardation punishes it, and when the curve steepens enough, VLCCs stop moving and become tanks at $30–60k/day.

The divergence that matters

Coal power is falling while coal use is rising

Those two facts sit in the table above and they are not contradictory. Two thirds of coal goes to electricity, and that portion has plateaued and begun to slip as renewables take load growth: coal's share of world electricity is now around 35%, the lowest since the IEA was founded in 1974. So the decarbonization story is real in the power sector and largely absent in the industrial one. Trade and the seaborne market

China imports more coal than any country ever has

International coal trade hit a record 1,544 Mt in 2024, of which China took 548 Mt, the largest figure any country has ever imported of anything comparable. Indonesia is the largest exporter at 555 Mt, then Australia at 363 Mt and Russia at 198 Mt, and those three are roughly three quarters of world exports.

This matters because it makes Chinese domestic policy a global price signal. When Beijing raises domestic production or draws down stockpiles, seaborne thermal coal prices fall worldwide, and the marginal Indonesian or Australian cargo is the one that clears. Chinese imports fell about 58 Mt in 2025 on high stocks and weak demand, and that single decision moved the whole market.

The forecast, and why to distrust it

A plateau, then a very gradual decline

The IEA has coal demand effectively flat through the late 2020s and about 3% below the 2025 level by 2030. That is a materially less aggressive decline than most climate pathways require, and it is the projection of an agency that has historically underestimated renewable growth and, in coal, has been closer to right than its critics.

Two things would break the plateau in either direction. Chinese electricity demand growth outpacing its own enormous clean build would push coal up; a faster than expected Chinese structural slowdown in steel and cement would push it down hard. Both are decisions made in one country, which is why the next section exists.

Overcapacity

1,100+ GW/yr of nameplate against ~600 GW of demand

Silver, not silicon

The material to watch

Each cell uses silver paste for contacts, roughly 10–15 mg/W. At terawatt scale that is a meaningful share of global silver supply, which is why copper metallization and thinner fingers are an active research front rather than an incremental optimization.

Enrichment as leverage

The dependency nobody priced before 2022

The uranium market
Concentration

Three countries, and one swing producer

Kazakhstan supplies roughly 40% of world mine production, Canada about 15% and Namibia about 12%; Kazakhstan, Canada and Australia together are near three quarters. That makes Kazatomprom the market\u2019s swing producer, and it behaves like one. Two prices

The spot market is a sideshow and the term market is the signal

Uranium has no meaningful exchange-traded futures market, so it is priced two ways. Spot traded from about $18 a pound in 2016 to over $100 in early 2024, back into the $80s, and above $100 again in early 2026. Term contracts, which is how utilities actually buy, moved to around $90 and reportedly higher in some deals.

A term price above spot is the unusual condition, and it says utilities under-contracted for a decade and are now securing supply into the 2030s. Because fuel is under a tenth of a reactor\u2019s cost, a utility will pay almost any uranium price rather than risk not having fuel, which is exactly why the demand curve is close to vertical.

The real bottleneck

Enrichment, not mining

The United States produces under 1% of world enrichment capacity and depended on Russian supply for a fifth of its reactor fuel. A US ban on Russian enriched uranium is in force with waivers narrowing toward the end of the decade, and Russia has at times restricted exports in the other direction.

In January 2026 the Department of Energy awarded about $2.7B to American Centrifuge Operating, General Matter and Orano Federal Services to rebuild domestic enrichment, including high-assay low-enriched uranium. The awkward arithmetic

US domestic uranium concentrate production fell 44% in the third quarter of 2025 to about 330,000 pounds from six operating facilities. That is a rounding error against roughly 60,000 tonnes of world demand, which tells you how little of the fuel cycle\u2019s value sits in the rock.

The strategic asset is not the deposit, it is conversion, enrichment and fabrication: the industrial steps that take eight to ten years and a treaty framework to replicate. Why offshore got more expensive while solar got cheaper

Three curves collided

Between 2021 and 2024 offshore wind absorbed a steel and resin cost shock, a rate shock that hit a highly levered asset class, and a vessel and port bottleneck at exactly the moment turbine ratings jumped from 8 MW to 15 MW. 13  /  Clean hardware

Hydrogen: an industrial feedstock being marketed as an energy system

The world already makes about 95 Mt of hydrogen a year and burns fossil fuel to do it. The honest version of the hydrogen transition is decarbonizing that existing demand. Everything beyond it competes with direct electrification and usually loses on round-trip efficiency.

Production

Grey, blue, green

Grey (SMR): methane and steam at 850℃. $1–2.5/kg, 9–12 kg CO₂ per kg H₂. Roughly all of today's supply.
Blue: the same plant plus 85–95% capture. $2–4/kg. Its climate credential depends entirely on upstream methane leakage, which is the part nobody measures well.
Green: electrolysis. Alkaline is cheap and slow; PEM is responsive and expensive; solid-oxide is efficient and fragile. 50–55 kWh/kg means power price dominates: at $30/MWh you are near $2/kg, at $80/MWh you are not close.

Moving it

Every option has a tax

Hydrogen has a third of methane's energy per cubic metre, embrittles high-strength steel, and liquefies only at −253℃ while boiling off in transit. Pipelines work for blends and for short dedicated runs. Ammonia is the practical ocean carrier, but conversion and cracking cost 25–35% of the energy. Salt caverns are the only proven bulk storage. Power to hydrogen to power round-trips at 30–40%, against 85%+ for a battery, so hydrogen storage makes sense for seasonal depth, not daily cycling.

Where it actually wins

Molecules that must be molecules

Ammonia and fertilizer, methanol, refinery hydrotreating, direct-reduced iron for steel, and long-haul aviation and shipping fuels. These need a chemical reductant or a dense liquid fuel, not electrons. Trucks and building heat are contested and mostly losing to batteries and heat pumps. The projects that reach financial close are the ones with an offtaker who already buys hydrogen today.

Policy shapes the entire cost curve

Subsidy design, not chemistry, will decide the winners

The US 45V credit pays up to $3/kg against a carbon-intensity ladder, with hourly matching and additionality rules that determine whether an electrolyzer can claim clean power. The EU's delegated act does something similar through additionality and temporal correlation. Japan and Korea buy on contracts-for-difference because they intend to import. Net effect: a $2/kg target that is achievable on paper anywhere the rules let cheap curtailed power count, and unreachable where they do not.

14  /  The electric system

The grid is a machine that must balance every second, and a market that clears every five minutes

Generation gets the headlines and the subsidies. The wires, the market design, and the queue decide what actually gets built. A power system has to hold frequency and voltage continuously, with no meaningful inventory, which is a constraint no other commodity system faces.

Physical layers

Four tiers, three voltages

Transmission at 115–765 kV, and up to 1,100 kV on Chinese ultra-high-voltage DC lines. High voltage cuts resistive loss, which scales with the square of current.
Substations step voltage down and provide switching and protection.
Distribution at 4–35 kV, then service transformers to 120–480 V.
Balancing authorities hold the whole thing at 50 or 60 Hz. Loss across the chain is roughly 7% for AC, against 3–4% per 1,000 km for HVDC, which is also the only option under water beyond about 80 km.

Invisible services

Inertia, reactive power, black start

A spinning turbine stores kinetic energy that resists frequency change for the seconds before controls respond. Inverter-based and provide none of that inherently, which is why high-renewable grids buy synchronous condensers, fast frequency response, and grid-forming inverter capability. Reactive power holds voltage and does not travel far, so it has to be produced locally. Black start capability, the ability to restart a dead grid without an external source, sits with a handful of hydro and gas units per region. None of these appear in a levelized cost comparison, and all of them are procured separately.

Markets

Three products, one asset

Energy clears day-ahead and in real time, usually at locational marginal prices that embed congestion and losses, so the same megawatt-hour is worth different amounts a hundred kilometres apart.
Capacity pays for being available, and exists because energy-only markets underprice scarcity.
Ancillary services pay for regulation, reserves, and voltage support.
Batteries earn across all three, which is why their business case looks nothing like a generator's.

The binding constraint

Queues and hardware, not generation

Interconnection queues in the US and much of Europe now exceed five years, and the volume of capacity waiting in them dwarfs what is operating. Large power transformers and HVDC converter stations run to 3–5 year lead times, constrained by grain-oriented electrical steel, skilled winding labour, and test-bay capacity rather than by raw materials. are the least glamorous and most decisive item in the energy transition. thesis

Merchant transmission has to earn its return from congestion spreads, which are exactly what a new line eliminates. Regulated rate-base transmission earns on capital deployed, which encourages building but not efficiency. Neither model reliably delivers interregional lines that benefit people in a different jurisdiction from the one paying, and that is the specific market failure holding back the highest-value transmission projects.

Who owns what

Three business models under one frequency

Vertically integrated utilities own generation, wires, and retail, earn a regulated return on rate base, and are compensated for capital rather than for kilowatt-hours.
Independent power producers sell into markets or under power purchase agreements, and carry merchant price risk that the cost of capital then prices.
System operators own nothing and dispatch everything, which makes them the only party whose incentive is reliability rather than return.
Where these three meet determines whether a country's grid expands. The same solar farm has a materially different levelized cost under each, because the discount rate is different, and the discount rate is most of the answer for a capital-heavy asset.

The premise worth correcting

Scale check

The whole sector is smaller than one customer

The five largest listed US utilities are worth roughly $560B combined. That is under a fifth of Microsoft, and a smaller number than several individual technology companies that are now queuing for gigawatt connections from these same utilities.

The monopoly bargain

Exclusive territory in exchange for an obligation

A distribution utility gets a franchise: no competitor may string wire in its territory. In exchange it must serve everyone who asks, at a price a state commission approves, and it earns a regulated return on the capital it puts into the ground. That is the deal Samuel Insull proposed in 1898 and it is still the deal.

The consequence is that a utility is compensated for spending capital rather than for selling kilowatt-hours, which explains a great deal of otherwise puzzling behaviour, including why efficiency programmes need a separate incentive to exist at all.

Who coordinates

Three interconnections, seven markets, sixty-six balancing areas

Physically the lower 48 is three asynchronous machines: the Eastern Interconnection, the Western Interconnection, and Texas. Commercially, about two thirds of US load sits inside an RTO or ISO, of which there are seven, and the rest is dispatched by vertically integrated utilities in bilateral markets, mostly across the Southeast and the West outside California.

Roughly 66 balancing authorities hold frequency in their own footprints. That fragmentation is why a transmission line benefiting two regions is so hard to build: nobody's rate base spans the benefit.

Texas

An island by choice, and the choice was jurisdictional

The Federal Power Act of 1935 gave federal regulators authority over electricity sold across state lines. Texas utilities responded by not crossing any. The wartime Texas Interconnected System proved a statewide grid could work inside one state's borders, and in 1970, after the 1965 Northeast blackout pushed the industry toward formal reliability organizations, it became ERCOT.

The independence was tested in May 1976, when a Central and South West subsidiary deliberately closed a switch and pushed Texas power into Oklahoma for a few hours, an episode known as the Midnight Connection, specifically to trigger federal jurisdiction. The litigation went on for years and Texas kept its independence. ERCOT today carries about 90% of Texas load and connects to the outside world only through direct-current ties to the Eastern Interconnection and Mexico, which move power without synchronizing the grids or, crucially, without making the sale interstate. El Paso, the upper Panhandle, and part of East Texas are not in ERCOT at all.

What that buys and what it costs are both visible. Texas built more wind and solar faster than any other state, partly because ERCOT's single-jurisdiction transmission planning produced the CREZ lines while other regions were still litigating. It is also an energy-only market with no capacity payment, so generators earn from scarcity pricing rather than from being available, and there is no neighbour to lean on when the whole state is cold at once. In February 2021 Winter Storm Uri took the system within minutes of an uncontrolled collapse that would have taken weeks to restore. The design and the outcome are the same decision viewed from two ends.

The pattern

Every regime was written by a disaster

State regulation in 1907 followed municipal franchise corruption. The 1935 Public Utility Holding Company Act followed the collapse of Insull's pyramid, which wiped out something like a billion dollars of small investors' money and made him a national villain. Mandatory reliability standards followed the 2003 blackout, twenty-eight years after the voluntary version had been judged sufficient. Texas weatherization rules followed Uri.

The corollary is uncomfortable: the interconnection queue and the transformer shortage are currently being managed by voluntary and incremental means, and on this record the binding fix arrives after the failure rather than before it. thesis

The reversal nobody planned for

Load growth came back

From roughly 2005 to 2020, US electricity demand was flat. Efficiency gains offset economic growth almost exactly, and an entire generation of utility planning, regulatory practice, and investment doctrine was built on the assumption that the load curve would never move again.

It moved. Data centres, electrified transport and heat, and reshored manufacturing arrived at the same moment, into a system whose queues, transformer supply, and workforce had all been sized for a flat future. That is the single most important fact about the present era, and the institutions have not caught up with it.

Nothing competes across the whole range. Flywheels and lithium cells are useless for a February in Germany; hydrogen caverns and reservoirs are absurd for a frequency excursion. Choosing storage means choosing a duration first and a chemistry second.

Seconds to hours

Lithium-ion, and why four hours

dominates because it is manufactured at automotive scale, so its cost curve is borrowed from a much larger industry. Cell prices fell roughly 90% over the 2010s. LFP chemistry, with no and no nickel, now takes a large share precisely because it is cheaper and safer rather than denser.

Four hours is the default duration for a reason: it covers the evening peak after solar output collapses, and capacity-market rules in several jurisdictions grant near-full capacity credit at four hours and much less below it. That is a regulatory artefact as much as an engineering optimum. Round-trip efficiency is 85–92%, degradation means an augmentation or replacement decision around year ten, and the economics depend on cycling revenue across energy, capacity, and ancillary markets simultaneously.

Hours to days

Pumped hydro, flow, compressed air, thermal

Pumped hydro is still over 90% of world storage energy by a wide margin: cheap per kilowatt-hour, 70–80% round trip, 80-year asset life, and almost impossible to permit. Closed-loop and abandoned-mine designs are the credible growth path.
Flow batteries decouple power from energy, since duration is a function of tank size, which makes them structurally better above about eight hours. Vanadium electrolyte cost is the obstacle, though it is leasable and recoverable.
Compressed air works where the geology does, and little else.
Thermal storage, in molten salt, rock, or hot water, is the cheapest way to store energy when the output you need is heat rather than electricity, which covers a surprising share of industrial demand.

Weeks to seasons

Fuels, reservoirs, and the honest answer

Only three things store energy at seasonal depth: a fuel, a reservoir, or a very long wire. in salt caverns is the leading candidate and round-trips at 30–40%, so it is economic only when the alternative is unserved load, not when it competes with a battery. Reservoir hydro already does this where geography allows, and Norway is the reason several North Sea interconnectors exist.

The fourth option is geographic: interconnection is not storage, but it substitutes for it, because a continent is rarely becalmed everywhere at once. That is usually the cheapest available flexibility and the hardest to permit, so the option most often chosen is the fifth one: keep the gas plants and run them a few hundred hours a year. thesis

Demand-side structure

How to read a claim critically

Four questions that catch most errors

1. Which convention? Primary energy on a substitution basis and on a direct basis differ by 30 EJ for the same year. Capacity and generation are different quantities. So are nameplate and firm capacity.

2. Which year, and is it an estimate? Most 2024 energy statistics were provisional until late 2025. Chinese and Indian figures revise more than most.

Critical minerals: mining is diversified, refining is not

The standard chart shows where minerals are dug up and concludes the risk is spread across many countries. The chart that matters shows where they are refined into usable chemicals and alloys, and it collapses onto one.

Lithium

Cyclical, not scarce

Australian spodumene and South American brine dominate extraction; conversion to battery-grade carbonate and hydroxide is roughly 60–70% China. Price went from about $80k/t at the 2022 peak to the low teens by 2024, halting projects that only worked at the peak. That whipsaw, not geology, is the supply risk.

~1.4 kg LCE per kWh · AU 47% / CL 24% mined

Cobalt

Being engineered out

DRC supplies roughly 70% of mined , much of it as a copper by-product, with artisanal production and governance exposure attached. Refining is 70%+ China. The response has been chemistry: LFP cathodes use no cobalt at all and now hold a large share of the global EV market. Demand growth is real but far below the 2018 projections.

NMC ~0.1 kg/kWh · LFP 0

Nickel & copper

Volume problems, not exotic ones

Indonesia went from a minor producer to over half of world nickel supply in under a decade via laterite processing, much of it coal-powered, which puts a carbon footprint inside the battery. is the deeper worry: no substitute in windings and cable, grades declining, and 10–15 years from discovery to production.

Grid + EV copper demand ~2× by 2040

Rare earths

The genuine single point of failure

Neodymium, praseodymium, dysprosium, and terbium for permanent magnets. Mining is roughly 70% China, separation about 90%, and finished sintered magnets a similar share. Export licensing in 2010 and again in the 2020s showed the tool works. Mountain Pass, Lynas Malaysia, and new separation plants in the US, Australia, and Europe are the response, at a decade of lead time.

EV motor ~1–2 kg NdPr · DD turbine ~200 kg/MW

The four real responses

Substitute, recycle, stockpile, re-shore

Substitution works fastest and is already the biggest lever: LFP removed cobalt, sodium-ion targets lithium, ferrite and wound-rotor motors avoid rare earths at a modest efficiency cost.
Recycling is technically strong, 90%+ recovery of nickel, cobalt, and lithium, but constrained by feedstock: the batteries have not aged out yet. It becomes material in the mid-2030s.
Stockpiling is fast and cheap for magnets and metals and does nothing about the missing capability.
Re-shoring is the only structural fix and the slowest: 5–8 years for exploration and permitting, plus a separation plant nobody wants to host.

Capital, not geology

Why the supply response is late

Mining spent 2013–2020 being punished for growth capex and rewarded for dividends. Boards learned the lesson. So the 2020s demand signal met an industry with disciplined balance sheets, longer permitting queues, and shareholders who remember the last cycle. Price spikes now get answered with buybacks first and mines second, and the IEA's estimate of the capital required through 2040 runs into the trillions.

The American structure

Tax equity, and why a solar farm needs a bank

US clean energy credits are non-refundable, and developers rarely have enough tax liability to use them. So for two decades the industry sold them: a bank or corporate takes an ownership interest, absorbs the credits and depreciation, and exits after the recapture period. Tax equity was roughly a $20B-a-year market and the reason a handful of large banks were structural gatekeepers to renewable deployment.

The 2022 Inflation Reduction Act added transferability under section 6418, letting developers simply sell credits for cash, and direct pay under 6417 for tax-exempt entities including municipal utilities and cooperatives. Both survived the 2025 rewrite. That collapsed a specialist financing bottleneck into something closer to a commodity market, and it is the single most consequential structural change in US energy finance in a generation. thesis

The current cliff

Three weeks ago, the rules changed

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed 4 July 2025, terminates the technology-neutral 45Y production and 48E investment credits for wind and solar unless construction began by 4 July 2026 or the project is in service by 31 December 2027. That begin-construction date fell three weeks before this page's data vintage, which makes the current moment an unusually sharp discontinuity: a safe-harboured pipeline racing to build, and very little visibility past it.

Storage was treated differently and keeps its credits until a phase-down starting in 2034. Nuclear under 45U, carbon capture under 45Q and non-wind manufacturing under 45X survived; hydrogen under 45V was cut short. New foreign-entity rules restrict who may claim or receive credits from 2026. And in June 2026 a court vacated Treasury guidance narrowing the 5% begin-construction safe harbour, on the grounds that the agency had not reasoned its way there. Read all of this as live rather than settled.

Regulated

Rate base, and the incentive it creates

A regulated utility earns an approved return on prudently incurred capital. Its cost of capital is low because its revenue is nearly certain, which is why the cheapest nuclear plant in the world is one inside a rate base with a supportive commission. It also means the utility is rewarded for spending, not for saving, so efficiency and third-party generation both need to be mandated or separately compensated rather than merely permitted.

Contracted

The power purchase agreement as the actual product

An independent developer's asset is not the plant, it is a twenty-year contract with a creditworthy offtaker. That contract is what allows 70 to 80% debt at reasonable spreads. It is also why hyperscaler PPAs repriced the sector so violently: an investment-grade technology company signing for two decades is a better counterparty than most utilities, and the cost of capital moves accordingly.

Merchant

Where the returns and the graveyards are

Uncontracted generation earns the market price and carries all the risk, so it demands double-digit equity returns. It is where the money is made when scarcity arrives and where developers die when it does not. Most of the offshore wind write-offs of 2023 and 2024 were contracted assets whose fixed prices met an unexpected cost of capital, which is the same failure from the other direction.

Permitting and siting: the actual reason things do not get built

Nothing on this page is constrained by physics in the way that public argument implies. The binding constraints are a queue, a transformer, and a permit, and of those three the permit is the one that has been the subject of a genuine legal revolution in the last eighteen months, in a direction almost nobody predicted.

What changed

NEPA was substantially rewritten by all three branches in about a year

Congress, in the 2023 Fiscal Responsibility Act, codified page limits, a one-year deadline for environmental assessments and two years for impact statements, and let project sponsors prepare documents.

The Supreme Court, in Seven County Infrastructure Coalition v. Eagle County on 29 May 2025, held 8-0 that agencies need not analyse the environmental effects of separate upstream or downstream projects outside their authority, and instructed courts to give agencies substantial deference on scope.

The executive, through Executive Order 14154, directed the Council on Environmental Quality to rescind its government-wide NEPA regulations, which it did by interim rule effective 11 April 2025 and finalised on 8 January 2026. Seven federal agencies issued their own replacement procedures in July 2025, and the OBBBA added a mechanism for sponsors to pay a fee for an expedited review.

What did not change

The permit was never only NEPA

NEPA is a procedural statute: it requires the agency to look, not to decide any particular way. Faster looking does not touch the substantive statutes that actually stop projects. Clean Water Act section 404 dredge-and-fill permits, Endangered Species Act consultation, National Historic Preservation Act section 106 review, and Clean Air Act permitting all remain, and all remain litigable.

More importantly, for the constraint this page keeps returning to, interstate transmission is sited by states. A single county or state commission can stop a multi-state line, federal backstop authority under Federal Power Act section 216 has been narrow and repeatedly litigated, and no amount of NEPA streamlining addresses it. The most consequential permitting problem in American energy is not federal. thesis

Gas has a federal path

FERC section 7(c) and eminent domain

An interstate gas pipeline applies to FERC for a certificate of public convenience and necessity under Natural Gas Act section 7(c). A certificate carries federal eminent domain. This is why gas pipelines are built across many states and electricity transmission usually is not: one statute created a federal siting route for molecules in 1938 and no equivalent was ever created for electrons.

The asymmetry nobody designed

Same wire, different regime

A transmission line inside one state faces one commission. The same line crossing three states faces three, each weighing local cost against benefits that accrue elsewhere, which is a structurally losing argument. This is the precise mechanism by which the highest-value interregional projects fail, and it is a cost-allocation problem wearing a permitting costume.

The honest counter-argument

Review exists for reasons

NEPA and its state analogues were written because agencies built things through neighbourhoods and wetlands without looking. Streamlining transfers power from objectors to sponsors, and the people who lose most often have the least ability to litigate. A serious permitting position has to say which projects it wants built faster and who bears the cost, rather than treating process as pure friction. thesis

25  /  What actually limits it

Four constraints that never appear in a cost model

Levelized cost is a per-megawatt-hour number and these are not. They are the things that decide whether a system is acceptable, survivable, affordable to the person paying the bill, and permitted to keep emitting. None of them shows up in the calculators above, which is a limitation of calculators rather than of the constraints.

Security · physical

A grid is the least defended critical system in a country

There are tens of thousands of substations, mostly behind a chain-link fence. In April 2013 a small group shot up Metcalf substation south of San Jose with rifles and came within a plausible margin of taking out power to Silicon Valley. In December 2022 gunfire at two Moore County, North Carolina substations blacked out 45,000 customers for days. Neither required sophistication.

The specific vulnerability is that large power transformers are bespoke, unmonitored, and on a three-to-five year lead time. Destroying a handful in one region is a materially different event from a storm outage, because there is nothing to install afterward. The mandatory NERC CIP standards that followed the 2003 blackout are largely about cyber and about information, not about steel and fences.

Security · cyber

The 2015 Ukraine attack is the reference case, and it was fifteen years ago in technique

In December 2015 attackers opened breakers at Ukrainian distribution utilities and left roughly 230,000 people without power, then returned in 2016 with a purpose-built tool for grid protocols. Both were recoverable in hours because the operators could switch manually. A more automated grid would have recovered more slowly, which is an uncomfortable feature of modernization.

The current concern is not sabotage but pre-positioning: US agencies have publicly described state actors establishing persistent access in critical infrastructure networks without acting on it, which is a capability held in reserve rather than a incident. The defensive asymmetry is stark, since an attacker needs one path and a utility with thousands of substations and a decades-old operational technology estate must hold all of them. est

Water

Thermal power is the largest water user in most industrial economies

Thermal generation, coal, gas and nuclear alike, is a steam cycle, and steam cycles reject heat into water or air. In the United States thermoelectric cooling has historically been roughly 40% of all freshwater withdrawals, comparable to agriculture. Withdrawal is not consumption, since most returns warmer, but the warmth is itself the regulated harm and the reason plants derate or shut in heatwaves, exactly when load peaks.

The transition changes this favourably and unevenly. Solar and wind use almost no water in operation. Hydrogen electrolysis uses about nine litres per kilogram, which is trivial next to the power it consumes. Data centre cooling is the new contested draw, concentrated in specific counties rather than spread nationally, and it is now a siting constraint in the American Southwest and in Ireland. est

Water · the other direction

Energy is also the largest user of water infrastructure

The dependency runs both ways. Pumping, treating, desalinating and heating water is a large and rising electricity load, on the order of a few percent of national consumption in water-stressed economies and far more where desalination dominates. Gulf states effectively co-produce power and water in the same plants, which couples two systems that then fail together.

Hydropower sits at the intersection and is the visible casualty: drought reduced hydro output sharply across China, the western United States and Latin America in recent years, and every one of those shortfalls was made up with coal or gas. Climate risk to hydro is a fossil demand risk, which is not how it is usually filed.

Energy poverty · global

Roughly 700 million people have no electricity and about 2 billion cook over fire

The access gap is now overwhelmingly sub-Saharan African and rural, and the clean-cooking gap is larger, more lethal, and receives a fraction of the attention. Household air pollution from solid cooking fuels kills on the order of millions annually, mostly women and children.

What that implies for everything above is a matter of scale rather than sentiment: bringing the entire unserved population to a basic modern standard would add a few percent to world electricity demand. Energy poverty is a very large moral problem and a very small emissions problem, and conflating the two has produced bad arguments on both sides for thirty years. thesis

Energy poverty · domestic

The retail bill is where all of this is finally settled

Every capital decision on this page eventually arrives as a line on a household bill, and the fixed-cost share of that bill is rising while the commodity share falls. That is a distributional machine: rooftop solar and efficiency reduce a customer's volumetric charges but not the network they still rely on, so the remaining fixed costs concentrate on customers who cannot install anything.

Energy burden, the share of household income spent on energy, runs into double digits for low-income households in wealthy countries, and millions of American households carry utility arrears at any time. Rate design is therefore not an accounting detail. It decides who pays for the transition, and it is decided in venues almost nobody attends. thesis

Capture · the honest case

CCS works, is expensive, and has a narrow set of real uses

Post-combustion capture is proven chemistry, roughly amine scrubbing of a flue gas, and the engineering is not in doubt. The problems are that capture costs $50–120 a tonne depending on stream concentration, that it imposes an energy penalty of 15 to 25% on the host plant, and that the capital is only justified where there is no cheaper alternative.

Which is exactly where it belongs: cement, where more than half the CO₂ comes from the chemistry of calcining limestone and no fuel switch touches it; steel where hydrogen reduction is not yet available; refinery hydrogen; and gas processing, where the CO₂ is already concentrated and separation is nearly free. In those applications it is the only known answer. thesis

Capture · the skeptical case

Scale, incentives, and what the tonnes have been used for

Operating capture capacity worldwide is on the order of 50 million tonnes a year against roughly 38 billion tonnes of energy CO₂, which is about a thousandth. Most of that capacity is gas processing and most of the captured CO₂ has historically been sold for enhanced oil recovery, producing more oil. The US 45Q credit, which survived the 2025 tax rewrite, pays more for dedicated storage than for EOR, which is the correct direction and recent.

The fair summary: capture is indispensable for a small number of industrial processes and has repeatedly been used to argue for prolonging combustion where cheaper alternatives existed. Both halves of that sentence are true and most public argument picks one. thesis

How a shortage of one alloy delays a gigawatt

Energy failures are rarely a failure of the headline technology. They are second- and third-order: a bearing, a forging, a vessel, a transformer.

Case study · offshore wind, 2021–2024

Four independent constraints, one schedule

1. Magnets. Rare-earth separation capacity outside China stayed flat while direct-drive turbine ratings climbed, putting magnet supply on allocation.
2. Forgings and bearings. Large-diameter bearings and Cr-Mo forgings have very few qualified suppliers, and EV and defence demand competed for the same furnaces. Lead times went past 18 months.
3. Vessels. Turbines outgrew the installation fleet. A new wind turbine installation vessel is $300–500M and 3–4 years, so 2025 capacity was set by orders placed in 2021.
4. Grid connection. converter stations and large power ran to 3–5 year lead times, and interconnection queues in the US and Europe stretched past five years.

Outcome: capital cost per kW rose 30–40%, and fixed-price offtake contracts signed pre-shock were cancelled or renegotiated across several US and UK auctions. Every individual constraint was fixable. They were not fixable simultaneously on the original schedule.

Diagnostic

Three questions per input

How many qualified suppliers exist, and what share does the largest hold? What is the lead time, and is it longer than my financing commitment? Can I redesign around it, and how much performance does that cost? Anything above 30% single-source with a lead time longer than the funding window is where the schedule will actually break.

The under-discussed one

Transformers and switchgear

Large power transformers now run 3–5 years, and the queue is not about copper or steel but about grain-oriented electrical steel, skilled winding labour, and test bays. No amount of generation capex helps if the interconnection hardware is the constraint, and this bottleneck applies equally to solar, wind, nuclear, and data centres.

And the new load

Compute as an energy consumer

were about 1.5% of global electricity in 2024, some 415 TWh, and the IEA base case has that reaching roughly 945 TWh by 2030 scenario, with individual campuses now requesting connections at hundreds of megawatts to gigawatts. That load is fast to build, credit-worthy, and willing to pay, which means it competes with electrification for the same interconnection capacity and the same transformers. It is the first genuinely new demand-side actor in decades.

What each number is, and how much weight it will bear

Every quantitative claim on this page carries one of five marks. The distinction matters more than the digits: a published statistic, an estimate assembled from ranges, an output of a model on this page, an editorial judgement, and a scenario are five different kinds of thing, and treating them alike is how confident-sounding analysis goes wrong.

The marks

Five levels of confidence

fact Published by a named institution for a stated year, reproduced without transformation.

est Assembled from published ranges, rounded, or reconciled across sources that disagree. Directionally reliable, not quotable to the digit.

model Calculated by the models on this page from the assumptions shown. Changes when you move a slider, which is the point.

thesis My judgement about what matters and why. Argue with it.

scenario An illustrative future consistent with published pathways. Not a forecast, and nobody should treat any 2050 number as one.

Known limits

What this page cannot do

The flow diagram uses the substitution convention for primary energy and is balanced to the exajoule so it renders cleanly. That rounding is a presentation choice, not a measurement.

The useful and rejected split applies sector-level end-use efficiencies in the style of Lawrence Livermore's energy flow charts. Real end-use efficiency varies by an enormous amount within every sector.

The storage heuristic is a curve fitted to the shape of published resource-adequacy studies. A real answer requires hourly dispatch across multiple weather years, and the honest gap between those two things is roughly a factor of two.

Firm cost is a screening adder, not a capacity-expansion result. It shows why comparing solar to nuclear on levelized cost alone is a category error, without pretending to resolve the comparison.

Prose without a mark is qualitative description. Where it makes a factual claim with a number, the number has a mark.
